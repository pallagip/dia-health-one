Research method · proposed framework · 19 November 2026

Compare opposing design hypotheses.

Dia Health One studies computational questions for glucose-responsive insulin. This proposed framework compares opposing design hypotheses before deeper calculations or external lab work. It reports no new result.

Scope: this article describes a proposal only. It is not an active or approved candidate-screening plan. It reports no candidate ranking or broad screen and does not authorize production simulation.
Begin with a question

Write down both sides.

A comparison is useful when each explanation makes a different prediction for the same defined question—and when the result could count against either explanation. For a hypothetical glucose-responsive insulin design, questions might include linker length, ring flexibility, glucose recognition, or receptor access.

Opposing hypotheses

Describe the alternatives.

For an important design choice, state what each direction might change and why. Record what observation would distinguish the explanations, along with assumptions and ways both could be wrong.

Matched comparisons first

Hold the comparison steady.

Start with designs that differ in a defined feature while sharing a common reference, conditions, method, and outcome measure. A hypothetical shorter-versus-longer linker comparison should hold the rest of the design and method steady. Neither option is ranked here.

Interactions later

Combine questions only when ready.

Test whether design choices interact only after their controlled comparisons are interpretable. Define the interaction question in advance and keep it separate from the effects already tested.

Questions for future comparison

Test assumptions at three levels.

These questions cover molecular design, the proposed glucose response, and the computational methods. A result in one area does not answer the others.

Structure

Compare the design choices.

A shorter linker may limit motion; a longer linker may change access and flexibility. A more rigid ring may preserve one shape, while a flexible ring may allow more movement. These are competing hypotheses, not recommendations.

Mechanism

Test the proposed response.

Glucose recognition is only one part of the question. Any future comparison should also consider reversibility, access to the insulin receptor, and whether other molecules could alter the proposed response. Stronger binding alone is not proof of better function.

Model

Check the method, too.

Different force fields, sampling methods, or scoring methods can produce different results. A disagreement should increase uncertainty. Do not select the method only because it gives a preferred answer.

Cost follows validation

Validate first. Ask again before scaling up.

Before moving to a more expensive calculation, check that the inputs represent the intended question and that the method is suitable for it.

  • Check the identity and provenance of inputs, and record any assumptions or transformations.
  • Check the method against appropriate reference cases and predeclared expectations.
  • Document limits, unresolved inputs, and disagreement between methods.

Validation is not authorization. A proposed next calculation needs its own review and explicit authorization. A favorable low-cost result does not by itself justify higher-cost work.

Keep the record complete

Counts and uncertainty stay visible.

Each stage should answer what happened to the work that entered it, including results that do not support the starting idea.

Track counts by stage

Report how many items entered, passed, failed, remained inconclusive, or were not assessed at each stage. Keep stage definitions and denominators clear.

Preserve negative results

Retain failed comparisons and rejected explanations with the same inputs, method details, and reasoning as supportive results. Do not remove a result because it is inconvenient.

State what remains uncertain

Separate observations from interpretation. Describe uncertainty in inputs, method, sampling, assumptions, and applicability, including where methods disagree or the question was not tested.

Evidence boundary

A framework is not evidence of performance.

This proposal reports no new result. Computation alone cannot establish measured binding, biological function, safety, efficacy, or clinical benefit. The intended future decision is what to test in an external lab, not whether an insulin candidate works. Any future work would need validated inputs, method review, authorization, and an evidence record.

Current records

Check the public record.

The public research workspace and status page remain the bounded record of evidence and program status. This proposal does not update them.
