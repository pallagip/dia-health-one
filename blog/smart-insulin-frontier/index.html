RESEARCH LANDSCAPE / 16 NOVEMBER 2026

Smart insulin is advancing on two different fronts

A glucose-responsive insulin programme has reached Phase 2, while several other designs have controlled blood glucose for days in diabetic animals. These are steps forward. They do not yet tell us which design will become the safest or most useful treatment.

Dia Health One · External research reviewPublished 16 November 2026~4 min read

Clinical testing: LY3938577

Eli Lilly is testing LY3938577 against insulin degludec in adults with type 2 diabetes who already use basal insulin. The Phase 2 study measures time in range as its primary outcome. The public trial record does not report results, so the trial shows clinical progress, not proof that the insulin works better or causes less hypoglycaemia.[1] Lilly lists LY3938577 as a glucose-sensitive insulin receptor agonist in its 2026 investor pipeline.[2]

Molecular switching: NNC2215

A different approach builds the glucose switch into the insulin molecule itself. NNC2215 uses a glucose-binding macrocycle and a glucoside. In the published study, its insulin-receptor affinity rose 3.2-fold as glucose increased from 3 to 20 mM.[3] A follow-up study found that paracetamol glucuronide, a common paracetamol metabolite, binds to NNC2215 more strongly than glucose and can increase receptor affinity at low glucose. That is a safety concern to solve, not a reason to discard the whole idea of a molecular switch.[4]

Depots: long duration in animals

Other teams are working on insulin depots that release more drug when glucose rises. One membrane-coated insulin crystal kept diabetic minipigs in the normal glucose range for more than three weeks in the reported study. A separate polymer complex maintained that range for 192 hours after one dose.[5][6] These results come from animal studies, not people. A long-lasting depot also raises a practical question: how quickly can treatment be stopped if the dose is too high?

Faster onset: formulation research

A fourth design tries to combine quick meal-time action with longer coverage. Researchers made a glucose-responsive formulation from modified insulin aspart and a boronic-acid polymer. In diabetic mouse and minipig models, it showed a swift onset and no discernible signs of toxicity in the reported studies.[7] It is an interesting formulation result, but it is not yet evidence that one injection can safely replace both basal and meal-time insulin in people.

What the evidence supports: There is no single winner. LY3938577 leads by clinical phase; the two depot systems stand out for reported duration in minipigs; NNC2215 remains a molecular-switch benchmark with a known metabolite-interaction concern. These results are not head-to-head comparisons.

The next useful evidence is straightforward: human trial results, repeatable responses to changing glucose, low activity when glucose is low, and a reliable way to stop or limit insulin release. Until then, the best candidate depends on the treatment goal.

This review discusses research by independent teams. It does not report a Dia Health One drug candidate, clinical study or approved treatment. The team’s own research remains non-clinical.

Two fronts, four approaches

Clinical progress and preclinical formulation research are advancing in parallel. The evidence level differs sharply by approach.

Clinical: LY3938577, Phase 2, results not posted.

Preclinical: molecular switches, membrane-coated crystals, polymer depots, and swift-onset formulations.
