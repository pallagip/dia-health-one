Why we test opposite ideas in smart insulin discovery

Our computational research plan compares molecular hypotheses to help laboratories choose what to test next.

Dia Health One · Proposed research frameworkPublished 19 November 2026~4 min read

Dia Health One is designing a computational discovery platform to help laboratories decide which glucose-responsive insulin candidates to investigate. Our latest research plan starts with a rule: for each important design choice, test a credible alternative.

A longer molecular linker might give a glucose-recognition component more freedom. A shorter linker might hold it in a more useful position. Both are plausible. The question is what evidence can distinguish them.

Why glucose recognition is only part of the problem

Glucose-responsive insulin is intended to adjust its activity as glucose changes. Published research on NNC2215 shows that molecular switching can produce glucose-dependent activity in laboratory and animal studies. That work provides an experimental reference for the field.[1]

It also shows why a promising mechanism needs to be challenged. A 2026 study found that NNC2215 interacted with paracetamol glucuronide, a metabolite of paracetamol. This interaction increased insulin-receptor affinity, particularly at low glucose. The authors reported that NNC2215 had not progressed into clinical development because of the potential interaction risk. These were laboratory and animal findings, with human effects modelled rather than clinically observed.[2]

For our research plan, the lesson is specific: evaluate how a design responds to glucose and what else could change that response. A favourable glucose-binding result is one part of a much larger question.

Give each assumption a competing hypothesis

We call this the opposite-bets strategy. It means making assumptions explicit and comparing alternatives under defined conditions. Three examples show how it works.

  • Shorter or longer linkers. Compare whether the distance between components improves the predicted geometry or introduces unwanted freedom of movement.
  • More rigid or more flexible macrocycles. Compare whether a constrained ring preserves a useful shape or prevents the movement required by the proposed mechanism.
  • Stronger or weaker glucose recognition. Compare binding behaviour across the intended concentration range. Affinity alone cannot establish response speed or reversible switching; concentrations and binding kinetics also matter.

The plan identifies 20 dimensions to explore. Its first six priorities are macrocycle rigidity, chirality, linker length, glucose affinity, conformational switching and insulin-receptor accessibility. These are proposed investigations. Each needs a controlled comparison.

Start with comparisons that can be interpreted

The first comparison should change one defined feature while keeping the others fixed. For example, two candidates could share the same recognition component, ring and attachment position, but use different linker lengths.

The next step is to examine interactions. Short and long linkers combined with rigid and flexible rings produce four combinations. A linker that appears useful in one architecture may behave differently in another. Comparing those combinations can help identify which design choices deserve more attention.

We also want the process to challenge the starting architecture. If an alternative mechanism provides a stronger basis for laboratory investigation, the platform should make that visible.

Use expensive computation selectively

The plan sets an ambition of around eight billion possible molecular designs. This is a proposed search space, not a count of molecules already screened or simulated.

At that scale, the workflow needs several filters. First, generate candidate structures and record their design history. Next, check chemical validity and remove duplicates. Use inexpensive predictions to prioritize a diverse subset, then apply suitable physics-based methods to selected candidates. The final step is to assemble a shortlist for external laboratory consideration.

AI would help choose which candidates to examine next by considering predicted properties, uncertainty and diversity. Detailed simulations would address specific questions about selected designs. The number of candidates entering each stage would depend on available resources and the reliability of the preceding methods.

Generated, chemically valid, screened, simulated and experimentally tested candidates must be counted separately. Each number describes a different level of work.

Challenge the models as well as the molecules

A conclusion can depend on the starting structure, chemical parameters or how thoroughly a simulation explores possible molecular shapes. Where appropriate, our plan calls for independent runs and comparisons between suitable methods. Published guidance for free-energy calculations makes the same core requirement: results must be reproducible under stated assumptions and interpreted against experimental evidence.[3]

If methods disagree, that disagreement belongs in the candidate record. It can reveal where additional sampling, better parameters or a laboratory measurement is needed. Negative results also need to remain searchable, so an unproductive direction is not repeatedly explored.

Make the next laboratory decision clearer

The intended output is a small set of candidates with an explanation of why each deserves attention. Each record should include its structure, design rationale, methods, predicted behaviour, uncertainty and unresolved questions. The plan suggests a possible shortlist of 20–50 candidates; this is a planning target, not a completed selection.

Computational results can help prioritize experiments. Laboratory evidence is still needed to establish glucose-dependent insulin activity, and appropriate experimental and clinical evidence is needed to establish safety and efficacy.

Our immediate focus is to define controlled comparisons, reference benchmarks and useful criteria for external testing. We welcome discussions with computational chemists and experimental groups working on molecular recognition, insulin biology or interference testing.
